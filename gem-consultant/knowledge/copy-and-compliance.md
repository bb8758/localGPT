# Copy guide
Voice: playful folklore. Template: [Hook] + [traditional belief, framed as lore] + [real facts: stone, size, material, treatment] + [call to action].
Example: "Black obsidian: folklore says it's a bouncer for bad energy. Whether or not the spirits listen, it's a gorgeous polished stone on adjustable cord."

## Legal basis
- [PRIMARY-ish, 2011-2013 CFR text via GPO] 16 CFR 23.22 says it is unfair or deceptive not to disclose a gemstone treatment when (a) it isn't permanent, (b) it creates special care needs, or (c) it significantly affects value. For online sales the disclosure belongs in the listing. Sources: https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/CFR-2013-title16-vol1/pdf/CFR-2013-title16-vol1-sec23-23.pdf ; https://www.ftc.gov/opa/2000/12/jewelryrule.htm . Confirm the current text on ecfr.gov.
- [SECONDARY] The Guides cover "gemstones and their laboratory-created and imitation substitutes" (16 CFR 23.0, https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/23.0). Lab-made stones may be called "synthetic," "laboratory-grown," or "laboratory-created," but never "cultured" (https://jckonline.com/?p=44437).
- [SECONDARY, UNVERIFIED severity] An Alibaba seller blog warns that "even subtle language like 'promotes calm' or 'enhances energy' can trigger ... platform penalties." Treat health/wellness wording as high risk.

## Rules
Do: write "traditionally associated with" or "folklore says"; disclose dye, stabilization, reconstitution, glass, or lab-made; give mm size and cord/metal type.
Don't: claim cures, anxiety relief, sleep, or health effects; guarantee outcomes; call imitation stones natural; use "sterling"/"gold" on costume listings.
Most commonly dyed (heuristic, verify per supplier): howlite sold as turquoise, dyed agate, "cherry quartz" (usually glass), "opalite" (glass).
